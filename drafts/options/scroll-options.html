Landscape on scroll

Five ways the home hero's landscape could transform as a visitor scrolls from the name down into Research and projects. Scroll through each stage: it holds in place while you scroll, so your scrolling drives it exactly as it would on the site. Scroll back up to run it in reverse.

All five draw the hero's own function, not a picture of one: the four topic bowls, the flat basin under the name and the opening descent, computed per pixel and solved with the site's heavy-ball solver. A and D are wireframes, B is ASCII, C and E are two other readings of the same surface. The flat basin under the name only exists to hold the name, so once the name has scrolled off it fills back in.

A

Mesh lift

The contour bands thin out into a wire mesh of constant-x and constant-y lines, then the mesh tips back and lifts into a height field. The topic minima sink into pits and the descent lies on the terrain. The most literal "wireframe".

A
  • On the siteRuns as the hero leaves the viewport and ends as a tilted mesh behind the Latest reel. Labels stay pinned to the projected pits; clicking the mesh still starts a descent.
  • Reduced motionThe flat contour map, unchanged. Nothing about the page depends on the tilt.
  • CostAbout 4,000 projected points per frame on a 2D canvas, redrawn only while the scroll position changes.
B

ASCII gradient

The map pixelates into character cells and becomes text in three readings of the same function, the order a solver needs them: height as glyph density, then the gradient as a field of arrows pointing downhill, then the descent written cell by cell into the nearest minimum. Each stage shows a real quantity.

B
  • On the siteEach stage gets about a third of the hero's scroll. Set in JetBrains Mono, already the site's face for data labels. Topic labels stay as pills over the glyphs.
  • Reduced motionThe contour map. A static ASCII still could replace it, but the bands carry the topics better.
  • CostAbout 5,000 glyphs per frame at desktop width, fewer on phones (larger cells). Cheap enough for a 2D canvas; a glyph atlas in WebGL if it ever stutters.
C

Terraces

Each of the 13 bands is a level set, so the map is already a stack. Scrolling tips it back and pulls the levels apart into a terraced model, like a laser-cut topographic relief: the topic minima become wells cut down through the layers. Not wireframe, but the closest to how the map is actually drawn.

C
  • On the siteSame placement as A. The terraces keep the bands' colours exactly, so the hero never changes palette.
  • Reduced motionThe flat map, which is the stack seen from directly above.
  • CostThirteen pre-drawn layer images, stacked by offset each frame. In the build it moves into the existing shader.
D

Dive

Scrolling is the descent. Scroll position sets the solver's iteration, and the camera follows the iterate down: from straight above the map, tilting into a wire mesh and closing in until it converges in a basin. Scrolling down the page means descending into the work.

D
  • On the siteNeeds a pinned hero to have room for the dive. Open: whether the basin it lands in should filter the project list below (today only a descent the visitor starts filters it).
  • Reduced motionNo camera move: the flat map with the converged path, as today.
  • CostA perspective mesh with depth fade. The strongest idea and the most motion; the camera move is what reduced motion must remove.
E

Scanlines

A coral scan line sweeps down the map, slicing it. Above the line every row becomes its cross-section f(x, y0), drawn as a ridgeline that dips at each minimum. When it reaches the bottom, the whole landscape is a stack of slices.

E
  • On the siteWorks without a pin: the scan line can track the viewport's top edge as the hero scrolls away, so the part still on screen is always being sliced.
  • Reduced motionThe contour map, or the finished ridgelines as a still; both show the topics.
  • CostAbout 45 polylines per frame. The cheapest of the five.

Placement

Separate from the look: how much scrolling the transformation takes and whether the hero holds still for it. The prototypes above are pinned so you can study them; the site need not be.

  • 1Scroll away

    No pin. The transformation runs while the hero leaves the viewport and finishes as its lower edge passes. Nothing holds the visitor; they may see only half of it. Suits A, C and E.

  • 2Short pin

    The hero holds for about 70% of a screen of scrolling, transforms, then releases into Research. Enough room for B's three stages and D's dive; a visitor who flicks past barely feels it.

  • 3Backdrop

    The landscape stays fixed behind the whole page at low contrast and keeps transforming as sections pass. The most ambient, but it competes with project thumbnails and text, so it fights the site's legibility.

Recommendation

B with a short pin (2). It is the option you asked about, and it is the only one where every stage is a quantity the solver uses: height, then gradient, then the path. It teaches the hero's idea as you scroll rather than only decorating it, and it lands on the same converged minimum the readout names.

Runner-up: E with scroll away (1), if you would rather nothing pins. It is the quietest, costs least and never holds the page.

Whichever you pick, the build keeps today's hero as the reduced-motion still, keeps clicking the map working in every state, and stops drawing once the hero is off screen. Reply with a letter and a placement number, or a mix (for example B's glyphs on A's mesh).
